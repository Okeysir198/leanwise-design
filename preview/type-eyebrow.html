Eyebrow

The shipped .lw-eyebrow, not a copy of its styles — this card renders the class, so a change to the motif shows up here first. Mono, uppercase, 0.14em tracking, and the mark's hexagon as the node.

Tracking is what makes it a label. At 12px an uppercase string without it reads as shouting; with it, it reads as a caption to whatever follows. The ink is the subtle tier, not the brand — only the node is cyan, so a page can carry many labels and still have one accent.

What the ratio column measures. White between words divided by white between letters, at the 18px the specimens above are rendered at. Letter white is sidebearing + tracking; word white is sidebearing + space advance + word-spacing + 2×tracking, the space taking a tracking step on each side, and the sidebearing is cap ink averaged across this specimen string rather than measured off one glyph. The figures are size-specific and do not transfer.

The untracked row carries ≫ rather than a number, and that is not a hedge. Untracked, letter white is a sub-pixel 0.63px, so the ratio is hypersensitive to how that cap ink is averaged: two defensible methods disagree by roughly a tenth on this row, while agreeing to within about 0.3 on the three tracked ones. So the tracked rows carry figures and this one does not — a precise number that moves with measurement technique would be the least honest thing on a card whose whole argument is measured. What matters is not in dispute: untracked, the face separates words far harder than it separates letters, and you read words without trying.

Tracking is what disturbs the balance. It opens every gap by one step. A letter gap starts near zero, so one step is a several-hundred-percent change; a word gap starts already wide, so the same step barely moves it. The ratio collapses to 5.2:1 — the letters catch up with the words, and the phrase starts reading as loose rubble rather than three words. That is the wide-tracked-caps defect, and it is a legibility problem, not a symmetry one: the gaps were always equal to each other.

So the word space opens too. word-spacing: var(--lw-word-spacing-caps) — +0.10em, lifting the word boundary's lead to 5.8:1. It is the letterpress rule: when you letterspace capitals, you open the word spaces to match. Tightening the space instead is the intuitive move and the wrong one — the third row above is that mistake, at 4.4:1, below even the untreated row. Every wide-tracked uppercase label in the system carries the pair: nav groups, table heads, KPI labels, chips, code-block languages.

The node re-points with the ground. On navy the hexagon takes --lw-brand-400 — the 500 cyan the light page uses sits at about 3.2:1 against this ground and goes muddy. The label's ink re-points too, but by a different route: it reads --lw-fg-subtle, which every dark scope already re-aims, so the text needs no brand rule of its own and the motif's rule holds on both grounds — one accent, on the node. Both sides are the same markup; only the tokens moved.

Two dark scopes, two tiers, on purpose. A dark marketing section (.lw-section.dark) turns the whole eyebrow brand-300, because there it is a display label with room to be loud. A dark app band ([data-band="dark"], which every template here sets) takes brand-400 on the node only, because an app screen carries many labels and can afford one accent each. The swatch below is the app-band marker, so this card shows the app tier.
